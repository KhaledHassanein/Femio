# Contraception safety source of truth

> Global product rule for Femio contraception education and routing. This document does not prescribe a method, determine eligibility, calculate pregnancy risk, or replace the current patient information supplied with a contraceptive.
>
> Medical reviewer: Mohamed K. · Review date: 23 July 2026 · Next review: 23 July 2027

## 1. Missed or late contraception

Missed-pill, late patch/ring, and late-injection actions depend on the exact product, formulation, timing, and jurisdiction. Femio therefore does not reproduce a universal decision tree or calculate whether a user remains protected. It links to the current instructions supplied with the exact product and directs the user to a pharmacist or contraception clinician. If unprotected sex may have occurred, Femio states that emergency contraception is time-sensitive and directs the user to a pharmacist or clinician promptly; it does not determine eligibility or residual pregnancy risk.

This avoids treating one regional rule as global. For example, the CDC's 2024 U.S. Selected Practice Recommendations use a 13-week DMPA reinjection interval and a two-week U.S. grace period, while also noting that WHO permits a different grace period. Product- and location-specific instructions control.

Sources:

- [CDC — U.S. Selected Practice Recommendations for Contraceptive Use, 2024](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/73/rr/rr7303a1.htm) (published 8 August 2024)
- [CDC — Combined hormonal contraceptives: late or missed doses](https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/hcp/usspr/combined-hormonal-contraceptives.html) (U.S. SPR 2024)
- [CDC — Emergency contraception](https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/hcp/usspr/emergency-contraception.html) (U.S. SPR 2024)

## 2. Return of fertility after stopping contraception

Do not promise a date for a period, ovulation, or pregnancy. After stopping pills or removing an implant or intrauterine method, available guidance does not describe a clinically important delay in return of fertility. DMPA is different: return can be delayed, varies widely, and can take up to about one year after discontinuation. This is a delay, not evidence of permanent infertility. Femio uses qualitative ranges and routes persistent concern to a clinician; it does not publish conception percentages, a median ovulation date, “occasionally 18 months/2 years,” or a universal no-period threshold.

Sources:

- [FSRH — Contraception for Young People, May 2019](https://www.fsrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-guideline-contraception-young-people-may-2019.pdf) (§5.4: no delay after POP/CHC, intrauterine contraception, or implant; DMPA delay up to one year)
- [FSRH — Progestogen-only Injectable Contraception, December 2014, amended July 2023](https://www.fsrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/progestogen-only-injectable-december-2014-amended-11july2023.pdf) (§11.3: DMPA return may be delayed up to one year)
- [NHS — What is the contraceptive injection?](https://www.nhs.uk/contraception/methods-of-contraception/contraceptive-injection/what-is-it/) (reviewed 8 February 2024)

## 3. Migraine with aura and combined hormonal contraception

The CDC U.S. Medical Eligibility Criteria 2024 classifies combined hormonal contraception as category 4 for migraine with aura: an unacceptable health risk if the method is used. Femio may show that guideline fact and tell the user it is important to discuss aura and contraception promptly with a clinician. It must not diagnose aura, tell an individual to start/stop/switch a method, or call a method personally “safe” or “unsafe.” The copy should not substitute disputed secondary descriptions such as “absolute ACOG contraindication” for the accessible primary CDC wording.

Medication and menstrual-migraine prevention content remains descriptive only. Femio does not supply a triptan, NSAID, supplement, or hormonal regimen dose or timing window.

Sources:

- [CDC — U.S. MEC 2024 summary classifications](https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/hcp/usspr/classifications-mec-contraception.html) (page updated 3 February 2026; classification from U.S. MEC 2024)
- [CDC — U.S. Medical Eligibility Criteria for Contraceptive Use, 2024](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/73/rr/rr7304a1.htm) (published 8 August 2024)

## 4. Lactational amenorrhea method (LAM)

CDC describes all three criteria as necessary: the user is amenorrheic, fully or nearly fully breastfeeding with intervals no longer than four hours by day or six hours by night, and less than six months postpartum. Femio explains the criteria but never outputs a protection verdict. When any criterion no longer holds, it directs a user who wishes to avoid pregnancy to current local contraception information and a clinician or pharmacist.

Pumping/expressing is not assigned a separate protection rule in the cited CDC appendix. Femio therefore asks about actual feeding intervals and supplementation and does not assert that pumping itself preserves or reduces LAM effectiveness.

Source:

- [CDC — Appendix G: Lactational Amenorrhea Method](https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/hcp/usmec/lactational-amenorrhea-method.html) (updated 26 September 2025; U.S. MEC 2024)

## 5. Folic acid when trying to conceive

WHO recommends 400 micrograms of folic acid daily from the time a person begins trying to conceive through 12 weeks of gestation. Femio may offer a reminder using that global public-health recommendation. Higher-dose regimens are clinician-directed because indications and local guidance vary.

Source:

- [WHO — Periconceptional folic acid supplementation to prevent neural tube defects](https://www.who.int/tools/elena/interventions/folate-periconceptional) (WHO eLENA intervention page, accessed 23 July 2026)
