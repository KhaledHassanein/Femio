# Menstrual and energy-availability safety thresholds

Status: medically reviewed  
Reviewer: Mohamed K.  
Review date: 2026-07-23  
Next review due: 2027-07-23  
Owner: `femio-medical-content`

This policy governs global user-facing wording for absent periods, heavy
bleeding, clot size, PBAC, functional hypothalamic amenorrhea (FHA), and
relative energy deficiency in sport (REDs).

## Absent periods

- Always consider pregnancy where biologically possible; Femio does not infer
  the cause of a missed period.
- ASRM's 2024 committee opinion defines secondary amenorrhea requiring
  investigation as more than 3 months without menses after previously regular
  cycles or 6 months after previously irregular cycles.
- For suspected FHA, the Endocrine Society suggests clinical evaluation when
  cycle intervals persistently exceed 45 days or amenorrhea lasts 3 months or
  more. FHA is a diagnosis of exclusion.
- User copy may say: “If you have gone 3 months without a period, consider
  arranging a clinical review. If your cycles were already irregular, guidance
  also defines 6 months without a period as requiring investigation. Seek
  advice sooner if pregnancy is possible, symptoms concern you, or you have
  signs of significant under-fuelling or illness.”
- These are conversation/evaluation prompts, not diagnostic thresholds.

Sources:

- [ASRM — Current evaluation of amenorrhea: committee opinion, 2024;
  Fertility and Sterility 122:52–61](https://www.asrm.org/practice-guidance/practice-committee-documents/current-evaluation-of-amenorrhea/)
- [Endocrine Society — Functional Hypothalamic Amenorrhea guideline, March
  2017](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/hypothalamic-amenorrhea)

## Bone-density education in suspected FHA

Do not tell a user to obtain a scan. The Endocrine Society suggests that
clinicians obtain baseline DXA after 6 or more months of amenorrhea, or earlier
where severe nutritional deficiency, other energy-deficit states, or skeletal
fragility are suspected. Femio may present this only as “a clinician may
consider bone-health assessment based on duration and risk factors.”

Source: Endocrine Society FHA guideline above, recommendation 2.7.

## REDs and low energy availability

- Explain low energy availability in plain language: dietary energy remaining
  after exercise may be insufficient to support health and performance.
- Do not display kcal/kg fat-free-mass/day figures, calorie targets, body-fat
  targets, or a single “safe/unsafe” cutoff to users, especially minors.
- The IOC 2023 consensus states that a single universal energy-availability
  threshold is fragile because responses vary between people, sexes, and body
  systems. REDs and low energy availability are not interchangeable labels.
- Femio never diagnoses REDs or prescribes nutrition/training changes; it
  routes to an appropriately qualified clinician and sports dietitian.

Source:

- [IOC consensus statement on REDs, 2023; British Journal of Sports Medicine
  57:1073–1098; doi:10.1136/bjsports-2023-106994](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/57/17/1073)

## Heavy menstrual bleeding and PBAC

- Use the NICE patient-centred definition: heavy menstrual bleeding is
  excessive menstrual loss that affects physical, social, emotional, or
  material quality of life. Do not require a numerical blood-loss estimate.
- A PBAC may be offered as a descriptive record to share with a clinician, but
  Femio does not display a ≥100 or ≥150 cutoff, diagnose heavy bleeding, or
  decide treatment. Instrument/version permission must be confirmed before a
  specific chart is reproduced.
- Symptom routing may mention bleeding longer than 7 days, needing a new
  product in under 2 hours, soaking a product hourly for several hours, or
  clots around 2.5 cm or larger as reasons to discuss bleeding with a
  clinician. These are not a complete triage rule.
- Urgent copy must remain symptom-based and separate from PBAC: very heavy
  ongoing bleeding with fainting, marked weakness, chest pain, shortness of
  breath, or other signs of instability warrants urgent medical assessment.

## Adolescents

ACOG states that adolescent cycles typically occur every 21–45 days and last
7 days or fewer. ACOG's patient information describes a typical period as
2–7 days. ACOG also recommends assessment for anemia, including serum ferritin,
when an adolescent presents with heavy menstrual bleeding.

Menarche and primary amenorrhea:

- Menarche typically occurs 2–3 years after breast development begins. ACOG
  reports that 98% of females have had menarche by age 15.
- Offer a calm clinician check-in if menarche has not occurred by age 15,
  within 3 years of thelarche, or if there is no breast development by age 13.
  These are evaluation prompts, not diagnoses or emergencies by themselves.
- Do not describe constitutional delay as the expected cause for an individual
  teen or publish cause-distribution percentages. The evaluation is intended
  to distinguish normal variation from a range of possible causes.
- A clinician may take a history, assess growth and pubertal development, and
  select blood tests or imaging based on the individual. Do not promise that an
  internal examination will never occur, that every test happens in one blood
  draw, that ultrasound will always be transabdominal, or that follow-up always
  occurs at one fixed interval.

Adolescent PCOS wording from the 2023 International Guideline:

- Irregular cycles are normal during the first year after menarche.
- From 1 to under 3 years after menarche, cycles shorter than 21 days or longer
  than 45 days meet the guideline's irregular-cycle definition.
- From 3 years after menarche to perimenopause, cycles shorter than 21 days,
  longer than 35 days, or fewer than 8 cycles per year meet the definition.
- More than 90 days for any one cycle after the first post-menarcheal year, or
  primary amenorrhea at age 15 or more than 3 years after thelarche, also meets
  the definition.
- Do not state that PCOS can never be diagnosed for 2–3 years after menarche.
  In adolescents, diagnosis requires both guideline-defined irregular cycles
  and clinical/biochemical hyperandrogenism after excluding mimicking
  conditions. Adolescents with only one feature may be considered at increased
  risk and reassessed. Pelvic ultrasound and AMH should not be used to diagnose
  PCOS during adolescence.
- Femio never applies these criteria to diagnose or label a teen; it provides
  screening awareness and routes concerns to a qualified clinician.
