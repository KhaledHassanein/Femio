# Sleep and Exhaustion After Birth

Exhaustion after birth is not only broken sleep. A body that has just been through pregnancy and birth is repairing, often short of iron, feeding a baby around the clock, and running on sleep chopped into pieces. Most of it improves as feeds spread out, and some tiredness is a symptom worth checking rather than a stage to endure. This is health information for planning, not a substitute for advice from your own clinician.

## Why it is this heavy

Newborns feed often, day and night, because their stomachs are small. Sleep in short blocks is not a sign you are doing anything wrong.

Blood loss at birth can leave iron low, which causes tiredness, breathlessness and dizziness that no nap fixes. Thyroid changes after birth can also cause exhaustion.

Pain, wound healing, and the sheer mental load of being responsible for a new person all take energy.

## What actually helps

Most sleep advice for new parents is unrealistic. These are the things that often help:

- **Sleep when you can, not when it is night.** A 40-minute daytime sleep is real sleep.
- **Split the night if there are two of you.** One person takes the first stretch, the other the second, so each gets one unbroken block.
- **Lower the bar on everything else.** The house can wait. Nobody remembers a tidy kitchen from this period.
- **Accept every concrete offer of help,** and turn vague ones into concrete ones. "Bring dinner Thursday" is a better answer than "thanks, we are fine".
- **Keep night feeds boring.** Low light, little talking, back down. This is for you as much as the baby.
- **Get outside in daylight once a day** if you can. It helps how the day feels, even when it does not add hours.

Sleeping arrangements are a safety question as well as a tiredness one, and safer-sleep advice differs between countries. Ask your midwife or health visitor about the guidance where you live rather than following what you read online.

## When tiredness is a symptom

Some exhaustion is not about sleep at all. Tell a clinician if you have:

- Breathlessness climbing stairs, a racing heart, or dizziness.
- Very pale skin, or lips and nail beds that look washed out.
- Tiredness that is getting worse rather than better after the early weeks.
- Feeling cold all the time, hair falling out heavily, or unexplained weight change.
- Sleeping and waking unrefreshed for weeks.
- Being unable to sleep even when the baby sleeps.

Low iron after a heavy blood loss is common and treatable, and a blood test can check it. [Heavy periods and iron-deficiency anaemia](/en/learn/heavy-periods-iron-deficiency-anaemia) explains what low iron feels like.

Thyroid problems can appear in the months after birth and cause exhaustion, and [ACOG's postpartum care guidance](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2018/05/optimizing-postpartum-care) frames the postnatal period as ongoing care rather than one visit, which is the point at which these things get picked up.

## Exhaustion and mood

Not sleeping and low mood feed each other, and they are hard to pull apart from the inside. One useful signal: being unable to sleep when the baby is asleep, night after night, can be a sign of anxiety or low mood as well as tiredness, so it is worth telling a clinician.

If you are lying awake checking the baby is breathing, or dread the night, read [your mood after birth](/en/learn/mood-after-birth). That is worth raising with a clinician.

## Feeding and sleep

Feeding often at night is normal in the early weeks, however you feed. Bottle feeding does not reliably buy longer nights, though it does make it easier for someone else to take a feed.

Babies do not sleep on a schedule at this age and cannot be trained to at this age. Advice promising otherwise usually costs money and delivers guilt.

## Going back to work, and the second wave

Many people find the tiredness peaks again around returning to work, when the day gets longer but the nights do not shorten. Plan for that rather than being surprised by it.

[NICE's postnatal guidance](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng194) expects wellbeing to be asked about at postnatal contacts, so use them. Bring it up at [your six-week check](/en/learn/six-week-postnatal-check) even if it feels like complaining about something ordinary.

## When to see a doctor

Contact your doctor or postnatal service for tiredness with breathlessness, palpitations, dizziness or paleness, for exhaustion that is worsening after the early weeks, or for being unable to sleep even when the baby does. Seek urgent care for chest pain, severe breathlessness, or fainting.

Ask for a blood test if you had heavy bleeding at birth and still feel wiped out weeks later. It is a reasonable request.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does newborn exhaustion last?

Feeds usually spread out over the first months, and tiredness often eases as sleep becomes less broken, though how long this takes varies a lot.

### Is it normal to feel breathless from tiredness?

Breathlessness is not just tiredness. It needs checking, especially after heavy bleeding.

### Should I nap during the day?

Yes, if you can. Short daytime sleep counts and is worth taking over chores.

### Can I train a newborn to sleep through?

No. Frequent feeding at this age is normal, and approaches that promise otherwise are not appropriate for newborns.

### Why am I awake even when the baby is asleep?

That pattern can be a sign of anxiety or low mood as well as tiredness, and it is worth telling a clinician.
