# What Care Options Can Help With Period Pain?

Heat, gentle movement, rest, and some pain medicines may ease period cramps. A clinician may also discuss hormone medicines or care for an underlying cause. The right choice depends on your symptoms, health history, pregnancy goals, and preferences.

Severe or worsening pain should not be managed with self-care alone. Ask for assessment when pain disrupts daily life. The main [painful-period guide](/en/conditions/painful-periods) explains when to seek help.

## Start with safe, simple comfort steps

Some people find relief from:

- A warm bath or shower.
- A heat pack or covered hot-water bottle on the lower abdomen or back.
- Gentle walking, stretching, or another comfortable movement.
- Enough sleep and a lighter schedule on difficult days.
- Relaxation, slow breathing, or massage.

Protect skin from burns. Follow the heat product's directions and do not sleep on an electric heat pad.

The [ACOG painful-period guide](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/painful-periods) lists heat, regular movement, and sleep as options that may help. These steps are choices, not a test of effort. Pain is not your fault if they do not help enough.

## Which pain medicines may be discussed?

Some anti-inflammatory pain medicines are called NSAIDs. They may ease cramps. They reduce the effect of natural chemicals that make the uterus contract.

These medicines are not safe for everyone. Ask a clinician or pharmacist before use if you have asthma or a stomach ulcer. Also mention kidney or liver problems, bleeding concerns, allergies, possible pregnancy, and all regular medicines.

Always follow the product label or personal clinical advice. Do not take two products with the same active ingredient. This article does not provide a dose.

Paracetamol may be an option for some people. A pharmacist can check which product is suitable for your age, health, and other medicines.

## What hormone options may be discussed?

Hormone medicines can make periods lighter or ease cramps for some people. Options vary by location. They may include tablets, a patch, a ring, an injection, an implant, or a device placed in the uterus.

These medicines can have effects beyond pain and are not right for everyone. The decision may depend on migraine, blood-clot history, smoking, other health issues, pregnancy goals, and personal preference.

If avoiding pregnancy is also a goal, ask a qualified clinician which method is suitable and how to use it. Femio's cycle estimates are not a reliable way to avoid pregnancy.

The [NHS period-problems guide](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/periods/period-problems/) describes medicine and hormone options that may reduce period pain.

## What if another condition causes the pain?

Care may change when another issue causes pain. This may be endometriosis, adenomyosis, fibroids, an ovarian cyst, or infection. The main [endometriosis guide](/en/conditions/endometriosis) explains one possible cause.

Review matters when pain starts later or becomes stronger. Seek care if it lasts beyond the period. Pain during sex, bowel movements, or urination also needs attention. Read [how painful periods are assessed](/en/learn/how-painful-periods-are-assessed) for common checks.

Surgery is not a routine first step for every person. When it is discussed, ask what the goal is, what alternatives exist, and what recovery may involve.

## How can you judge whether an option helps?

Agree on a fair review period with a clinician. Track only a few useful details:

- Pain strength and duration.
- What you used and when.
- Side effects.
- Bleeding changes.
- Sleep or missed activities.
- Whether the result met your goal.

The guide to [tracking your menstrual cycle](/en/learn/how-to-track-menstrual-cycle) can help you keep the record short. If flow is a major concern, read the [heavy-period guide](/en/conditions/heavy-periods).

Do not keep using an option that causes a serious reaction. Ask when mild side effects may settle and which signs mean you should stop and seek advice.

## Questions for shared decisions

Useful questions include:

- What cause best fits my pain?
- What is the goal of this option?
- What benefits and side effects should I expect?
- Does it fit my health history and pregnancy goals?
- What should I do if it does not help?
- When should we review the plan?

You can ask for another option. Personal comfort, cost, access, and the ability to use something consistently all matter.

## When to see a doctor

Book an appointment when cramps stop normal tasks or pain gets worse. Seek care if self-care no longer helps enough. Also report pain between periods, pain during sex, bowel or bladder pain, heavy bleeding, unusual discharge, fever, or fertility concerns.

> **Seek care now:** Get urgent medical help for sudden severe pelvic or abdominal pain, fainting, severe dizziness, fever with worsening pain, or heavy bleeding with weakness or trouble breathing. Contact local emergency services or go to the nearest emergency department.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does stronger pain mean I should take more medicine?

No. Follow the label or personal advice. Taking extra can cause harm.

### Is heat safe for cramps?

It may help, but protect your skin and follow the product directions.

### What if the first option does not help?

Return for review. The cause, choice, or plan may need to be reconsidered.

### Is surgery always needed for severe period pain?

No. It is considered only in selected situations after discussion of the goals, risks, and alternatives.
