# What Can You Expect During a Typical Pregnancy?

Pregnancy is usually described in three trimesters, but symptoms, appointments, and tests vary by person and healthcare system. Early and regular prenatal care gives you a place to ask questions, review medicines, and check your health and the baby's development. Even a pregnancy that has been straightforward can develop urgent warning signs, so know when to seek help.

## What happens during pregnancy?

Pregnancy begins when a fertilised egg implants in the lining of the womb (uterus). Over the following months, the placenta develops, the fetus grows, and the body changes to support pregnancy and birth.

Gestational age is the way clinicians date a pregnancy. It is usually counted from the first day of the last menstrual period, so the count begins before conception. This is an estimate, and an ultrasound may adjust the date.

You can use Femio's [gestational-age calculator](/en/calculators/gestational-age) or [due-date calculator](/en/calculators/due-date) for a general estimate. A clinician or maternity team confirms which date to use for care.

## What may happen in the first trimester?

The first trimester runs from the start of pregnancy through the early weeks. Common changes can include:

- A missed period.
- Nausea or vomiting.
- Tiredness.
- Breast tenderness.
- Needing to urinate more often.
- Food or smell changes.
- Mild cramping or light spotting.
- Mood changes.

Symptoms are not a measure of pregnancy health. Some people have many symptoms and others have few.

Contact a maternity service after a positive test so prenatal care can begin. The [ACOG prenatal-care guide](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/prenatal-care) says the first visit may cover health and family history, medicines, vaccines, past pregnancies, support needs, and the date of the last period.

Do not stop a prescribed medicine without speaking with the prescriber. Bring all medicines, vitamins, supplements, and herbal products to the review.

## What may happen in the second trimester?

The second trimester is often when the abdomen becomes more visible and fetal movement is first noticed. The timing of first movement varies, especially between first and later pregnancies.

Other changes can include:

- Skin changes.
- Back, hip, or pelvic discomfort.
- Indigestion or constipation.
- Nasal stuffiness or gum bleeding.
- Leg cramps.
- Changes in sleep.

An anatomy ultrasound may be offered during this part of pregnancy, depending on local care. Other tests are based on gestational age, health history, and local guidance.

Ask your maternity team how to learn your baby's usual movement pattern when movement becomes regular. There is no single number that fits every pregnancy. A clear reduction or stopping of movement needs prompt medical advice.

## What may happen in the third trimester?

In the third trimester, the fetus gains size and prepares for birth. You may notice:

- More pressure in the pelvis.
- Shortness of breath with activity.
- Needing to urinate often.
- Trouble finding a comfortable sleep position.
- Swelling in the feet or ankles.
- Practice tightenings of the womb.
- Back or pelvic discomfort.

Your care team may discuss birth preferences, feeding, support after birth, and signs of labour. Plans can change, and a change is not a personal failure.

Sudden swelling of the face or hands, severe headache, vision changes, chest pain, or trouble breathing are not symptoms to monitor in an app. Seek urgent care.

## What does prenatal care involve?

Prenatal, or antenatal, care is health care during pregnancy. The [WHO antenatal-care guidance](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/WHO-RHR-16.12) describes care as more than tests. It includes respectful communication, health information, emotional support, and timely checks.

Visits may include:

- Blood pressure checks.
- Urine or blood tests.
- Review of medicines and vaccines.
- Discussion of food, movement, sleep, and substance use.
- Ultrasound scans.
- Checks of fetal growth and movement.
- Screening choices.
- Questions about safety, mood, and support at home.

The schedule varies by country, health needs, and whether the pregnancy needs extra monitoring. Attend the visits offered and ask what each check is for.

Screening estimates whether a condition may be more likely. It does not confirm that the condition is present. Your clinician should explain choices, limits, and possible next steps.

## Food, movement, and medicines

Pregnancy nutrition does not need to be perfect. Aim for varied foods that are safe in pregnancy and fit your culture, budget, allergies, and health needs. Ask local maternity services about foods to avoid because advice can differ by country.

Movement is often safe in a straightforward pregnancy, but the right type and level depend on health, symptoms, and prior activity. Ask your clinician if you have pain, bleeding, dizziness, a heart or lung condition, or another concern.

Do not use medicine, supplements, or herbal products simply because they are labelled “natural.” A pharmacist, doctor, or midwife can check them. Femio does not provide medicine doses.

## Emotional well-being during pregnancy

Pregnancy can bring joy, worry, mixed feelings, or distress. Mood and anxiety concerns can begin during pregnancy, not only after birth. Tell your care team if low mood, fear, intrusive thoughts, or sleep problems are affecting daily life.

> **Seek care now:** If you may harm yourself or someone else, feel unable to stay safe, or are in immediate danger, contact local emergency services or go to the nearest emergency department. Femio's global [Need help now](/en/help-now) page can help you find the next safe step.

## When to see a doctor

Contact a clinician or maternity service after a positive pregnancy test to arrange care. Ask sooner if you have a long-term health condition, take regular medicines, had concerns in a past pregnancy, or are unsure where to access care.

Contact your maternity team the same day for symptoms that worry you, even when they are not listed below. Tell any clinician that you are pregnant and how many weeks you may be.

> **Seek care now:** Get immediate medical help for vaginal bleeding that is more than spotting; severe or lasting abdominal pain; a severe headache that does not ease; vision changes; chest pain; trouble breathing; fainting; fluid leaking from the vagina; fever with feeling very unwell; or a clear reduction or stopping of the baby's movement. The [CDC warning-sign list](https://www.cdc.gov/hearher/maternal-warning-signs/index.html) also includes severe swelling of the face or hands and severe nausea or vomiting with inability to keep fluids down. Contact local emergency services or go to the nearest emergency department.

This list cannot cover every urgent problem. If something feels seriously wrong, seek care rather than waiting for an app entry or the next appointment.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a due date exact?

No. It is an estimate used to plan care. A dating ultrasound may lead the maternity team to use a different date.

### Is it concerning to have few pregnancy symptoms?

Symptoms vary and can change. Their number or strength cannot confirm how a pregnancy is progressing. Contact your maternity team about a sudden change that worries you.

### When should fetal movement become regular?

Timing varies. Your maternity team can explain what to expect for your pregnancy. Once you know your baby's pattern, seek prompt advice for a clear reduction or stopping of movement.
