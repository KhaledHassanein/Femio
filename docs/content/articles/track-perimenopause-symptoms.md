# How Should You Track Perimenopause Symptoms?

Track the changes that matter most to you, such as period dates, flow, hot flushes, sleep, mood, headaches, or vaginal and bladder symptoms. Add a short note about daily impact. A simple record can support a clinical conversation, but it cannot confirm perimenopause or rule out another cause.

Choose a small set you can record consistently rather than trying to track everything.

## Start with your main question

Ask what you want the record to help with. You may want to understand changing periods, explain night sweats, compare sleep and mood, or prepare to discuss care.

Pick three to six items linked to that goal. You can change them later. Tracking should serve you, not become another burden.

The main [perimenopause guide](/en/learn/perimenopause-explained) explains the life stage and common changes.

## How should you record periods?

Record:

- The first and last day of bleeding.
- Light, medium, or heavy flow.
- Spotting between periods.
- Bleeding after sex.
- Clots, flooding, or leaks.
- Pain and dizziness.

Cycles may become closer together, farther apart, or less predictable. Use the same rule for Day 1 each time. The guide to [tracking your menstrual cycle](/en/learn/how-to-track-menstrual-cycle) explains a consistent method.

Do not assume every bleeding change is due to perimenopause. Read the [heavy-period guide](/en/conditions/heavy-periods) when flow affects daily life.

## Which body symptoms may be useful to track?

The [NHS symptom guide](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/menopause-and-perimenopause/symptoms/) lists a wide range of possible changes. You might record:

- Hot flushes and night sweats.
- Sleep quality and waking.
- Headaches or migraine changes.
- Joint or muscle aches.
- Palpitations, or a fast and noticeable heartbeat.
- Vaginal dryness or pain during sex.
- Bladder urgency or repeated urine infections.
- Skin or hair changes.

Record timing, strength, duration, and effect. “Woke three times with sweats and felt too tired to drive” is more useful than “bad night.”

## How should you track mood and thinking changes?

You may notice low mood, anxiety, irritability, poor focus, or memory concerns. Use neutral words and one simple scale. Add whether the change affected work, relationships, sleep, or self-care.

Mood symptoms can have many causes and may overlap with depression, anxiety, thyroid issues, poor sleep, medicine effects, or major life stress. Tracking cannot decide the cause.

Seek help promptly if mood changes feel unsafe or you cannot manage daily life.

## How long should you track?

A few weeks may show the effect of frequent symptoms. Several months may better show changing cycles. Do not delay care just to collect more data.

Review the record at a set interval, such as once a week. Daily review can make normal variation feel more alarming. Look for patterns without forcing one.

## How do you make an appointment summary?

Keep the full record, but make a one-page summary with:

- The dates covered.
- Shortest and longest cycles.
- Main bleeding changes.
- The three symptoms with the greatest impact.
- Changes from your earlier pattern.
- Medicines and supplements.
- What you want help with.

The [NICE menopause guidance](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng23/chapter/Recommendations) says care should be tailored to the person and adapted as symptoms change. Your summary should show what matters to you, not just a list of scores.

## How can Femio estimates change?

Cycle estimates become less certain when periods vary. Femio should show a range rather than claim one exact future date. A “late” label cannot explain why a period did not arrive.

Pregnancy can still occur during perimenopause. A calendar cannot confirm whether ovulation happened.

**These dates support conception planning and awareness. They are estimates, not a reliable way to avoid pregnancy.**

## What should you ask a clinician?

Ask which changes fit perimenopause and what else may need checking. Ask what each care option may help, what risks apply to you, and when the plan should be reviewed.

Read the [menopause overview](/en/learn/menopause-explained) and [menopause care-options guide](/en/learn/menopause-care-options) before the visit if useful.

## When to see a doctor

Book an appointment when symptoms affect sleep, work, mood, sex, or daily comfort. Seek advice for bleeding between periods, after sex, after 12 months without a period, or before age 45 when menopause-like changes begin.

> **Seek care now:** Get urgent medical help for very heavy bleeding with fainting, severe weakness, chest pain, or trouble breathing. Sudden severe pelvic or abdominal pain also needs prompt assessment. Contact local emergency services or go to the nearest emergency department.

## Frequently asked questions

### Must I track every symptom?

No. Choose a small group linked to your main concern.

### Can tracking confirm perimenopause?

No. It can show patterns and support a clinical discussion.

### Should I wait months before seeking care?

No. Bring whatever record you have when symptoms worry you.

### Are cycle predictions exact during perimenopause?

No. Changing cycles make calendar estimates less certain.
