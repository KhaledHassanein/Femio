# Changelog
All notable changes to Femio V2.

## [Unreleased]
### Changed
- **My patterns.** From the Calendar, see your cycle lengths side by side and what you logged most, counted by cycle ("Cramps during your period: 5 of 6 cycles"). When your cycle lengths vary a lot, one calm line suggests mentioning it at your next visit. In pregnancy and menopause, see how often you logged each thing in the last 90 days. femio shows counts only, never a cause or a verdict.
- **My medicines.** Add a medicine from your prescription, set the times you take it, and mark each dose taken or not taken. femio can remind you at those times and the day before your refill day, with your own words on the reminder if you want them. Doses still to mark show on Today. femio gives no dose, catch-up or interaction advice, and keeps no schedule for birth control.
- **Documents and photos in your record.** Attach a PDF or take a photo of a report on any entry. Files are sealed on your phone like the rest of your record, PDFs open inside femio, and you can rename, share or delete them, or find them all under My documents. Your backup carries them (up to 100 MB). femio asks for no camera or storage permission: your own camera app and file picker hand over only what you choose.
- **My health record.** From the "+" sheet you can now keep your visits, lab results, scans, medicines, conditions, allergies, procedures, pregnancies and notes in one timeline on your phone. Each entry shows when it happened, when you added it and whether you edited it; values stay exactly as you typed them and femio never judges them. It travels inside your encrypted backup.
- **A home-screen widget for Android.** Add femio to your home screen and it shows only the name and "Open femio", with no health words, so anyone who sees your phone learns nothing. If you want more, turn on "Show details on the home-screen widget" in Settings: it then shows your pregnancy week, or your next period as a range of days. Turn the switch off and the widget goes back to the name at once. The widget holds only those two lines, never your records, and nothing is sent anywhere.
- **A "+" in the middle of the bottom bar.** One tap opens a sheet to log your flow or mood right there, tell femio anything in your own words, ask femio a question or prepare questions for a visit; everything else is one more tap into the full log. It replaces the floating Ask femio button.
- **Today shows the fertile window that is coming**, not one that has already ended.
- **The calendar shows your whole cycle.** Your period now fills the days you said it lasts, this cycle's fertile window and likely ovulation day stay on the calendar after they pass, and each predicted period has its fertile window before it. If you told femio a regular cycle length, you see the next three periods (dashed, as estimates) instead of one.
- The welcome screen and the four Play listings now open with "AI-assisted women's health & wellness", the same words as the website. Ask femio's intro and the visit summary say what they are without clinical wording (R7.7).
- **Clearer, safer wording across the guides and the app, in four languages.** The 37 week-by-week pregnancy guides now list thoughts of harming yourself or the baby as an emergency sign and say to call straight away (not the same day) if the baby's movements slow down, stop or change. After birth, soaking a pad in an hour or an egg-sized clot is marked as emergency care. Unsourced numbers are hedged rather than stated, and the wordmark is written "femio" everywhere.
- **"Meet femio" describes the app as it is**: a private Android app with no account, records kept on your phone, a PIN lock and your own backup file. The old page still described a web app with accounts and sync.
- **The guide on fasting in Ramadan during pregnancy is withdrawn** in all four languages; its address now opens the pregnancy basics guide.
- **Ask femio answers far more questions.** Its library of trusted sources grew from 89 to 286 entries: more passages from the US Office on Women's Health, CDC and NIH on trying to conceive, each stage of pregnancy, breastfeeding and the months after birth, periods, menopause and everyday health, plus NHS reading and Saudi Ministry of Health pages in Arabic. It also understands more ways of asking, in all four languages. More birth-control phrasings in Egyptian Arabic, French and Spanish now get the fixed answer.
- **"Do I have…?" questions get an honest first line.** When you ask Ask femio whether you have a condition, it first says that only a doctor can tell, then shows what trusted sources say about it.
- **Fewer false alarms in Arabic.** Asking about a negative blood type in pregnancy, or how long bleeding lasts after birth, no longer opens a warning card; heavy bleeding, soaking a pad every hour and every other warning sign still do.
- **No ads in the app, and no Google Analytics or Meta pixel on the website** (ADR-080). The app no longer contains an ads SDK or uses the advertising ID. The website counts visits with Vercel Web Analytics, which sets no cookie and stores no internet address; ads on article and topic pages still appear only after you allow them.
- **Cloud boost asks for consent per use and keeps a dated record.** One box for general questions and one for tips from four broad facts, nothing pre-ticked; the screen says who receives the information, where, for how long, and what Google still sees. The app keeps the date and the version of what you agreed to, shows it, and records when you turn it off. A restored backup asks again.
- **Write a note on any day, and take your logs out as a spreadsheet.** The Log screen has a Note box that saves as you type and stays on your phone, in your backup too; a day with only a note now shows as logged on the calendar. In Settings, "Export as spreadsheet (CSV)" saves one row per logged day, in your language, to the place you choose. Unlike the backup, that file is not encrypted.
- **A rewritten privacy policy and a new consumer health data policy**, in four languages, listing everything that can leave your phone.

### Added
- **Two new calculators:** a period calculator that shows your next three periods as honest ranges based on how much your own cycles vary, and a pregnancy test calculator that tells you the day a home test is most reliable (from the NHS's guidance).
- **Calculator results you can see:** your fertile window, ovulation and next periods are drawn on a calendar, and pregnancy results show your week and trimester on a bar.
- **Femio can now show you your own patterns, three ways.** When things tend to happen in your cycle, which things you tend to log on the same day, and whether you have been rating something higher or lower lately. Every one comes with how much it rests on — "together in 4 of 6 cycles" — and how sure Femio is, because a pattern is the kind of evidence that is easy to over-read. **Nothing here interprets anything.** It never says one thing causes another, it never predicts, and it never says a symptom is getting *worse* — only that you rated it higher, which is a fact about what you typed rather than a judgement about your health. It waits for about three cycles before saying anything at all: fewer than that is a coincidence, and calling it a pattern would be a guess.
- **Reminders, and you decide every part of them.** Five things Femio can remind you about — your next expected period, a nudge to log, an appointment coming up, something you take, your cycle summary — and all of them start off. **The wording stays private by default:** a notification says "Femio — open the app" and nothing about your health, which matters if your phone shows notifications on the lock screen, and the settings screen shows you the exact words rather than promising they are discreet. You set your own quiet hours. **Femio never sends streaks, never counts the days you did not log, and never tells you a period is late** — a reminder is an offer, not a score.
- **When you share with a partner, you name them first.** Sharing now starts with who it is for, in your own words. Only you ever see that name: the other person is never shown it and cannot change it. It also means the record of what you agreed to says who you agreed to share it with, which is what the law actually asks for.
- **You can share part of your record with a partner — and only the part you choose.** Seven things can be shared, each on its own: when your next period is expected, your recent period dates, where you are in your cycle, how many weeks pregnant you are, the symptoms you log, the medicines you take, and your charting. Everything starts off. **There is deliberately no "share everything" button.** Sharing runs for six months and you can stop it in one tap, with no confirmation step and — this is the point — **without the other person being told**. Their view simply returns nothing, exactly as it would if you had never shared. **They cannot see what you left out, either**: a category you did not share is invisible to them rather than shown as hidden, so granular sharing never hands anyone a list of what you kept private. Three things can never be shared at all, and Femio does not offer them even as an unticked box — your questionnaire scores, anything from "Need help now?", and the questions you write down for an appointment. Sharing is not available under 18, and if you have not told Femio your age it treats you as under 18. It works by a one-time code you give to someone with their own Femio account, never a link that can be forwarded.
- **If your cycles vary, Femio now tells you what they actually did.** Instead of only widening its estimate and saying less, your dashboard shows the range your own cycles have run and how many they are drawn from — the first thing a clinician asks. Nothing is graded: there is no ideal amount of variation.
- **A summary of the cycle that just ended.** How long it ran start to start, how that compares with your own usual, the days you logged bleeding, and the symptoms you noted. No score and no verdict — a longer cycle is not a worse one.
- **Femio can now show you your own pattern.** If you have logged a symptom across at least three cycles, your dashboard says which days of your cycle it fell on and in how many cycles — counted from your entries, with nothing read into it. It waits until there is enough to be worth saying.
- **Your dashboard now explains the phase you are in.** What is happening around ovulation, in the luteal phase, and the rest — drawn word for word from Femio's own clinician-reviewed guide, with the NHS and ACOG sources it cites, and a link to read the whole thing. Femio still says nothing about a phase it cannot place.
- **A Tools tab, with everything Femio can do in one place.** The doctor-visit summary, fertility charting, appointments, medications and the screeners were all built and working but reachable from nowhere. Nothing on that screen is hidden based on the life stage you chose — your dashboard decides what to lead with, Tools is where the rest stays.
- **The doctor-visit summary now separates what you recorded from what Femio worked out.** Your logged periods, symptoms, medications and notes appear as your own entries; anything Femio estimated — your average cycle length, the next expected window — sits in its own clearly marked section, and says whether the average came from the cycle length you gave us at setup or from the periods you have actually logged.
- **Femio now asks how long your cycle usually is, during setup.** It uses your answer for the first cycle or two, then quietly switches to what your own logged periods actually show — and it always tells you which of the two you are looking at. You can skip the question, and Femio will simply wait for your data rather than assuming a number for you.
- **Your dashboard now shows where you are in your cycle**, as a coloured strip with today marked on it — period, then the days before and around ovulation, then the days after. Femio names the phase only when your own cycles are regular enough to place one; when they vary, it says so instead of guessing.
- **Setting up takes two taps.** Femio asks when your last period started, then the one before it, and predicts from there. It used to ask you to log "a couple of periods" and then only offer a button that logs today.

### Fixed
- **Search works again, and answers questions.** Search had stopped returning articles while the database was restricted, because every letter typed asked the database. It now searches a list built into the website, so results appear instantly and never depend on the database. You can also type a question in your own words, such as "bleeding after birth", rather than only an article's name. On the Arabic site, English article titles no longer show their question mark at the wrong end.
- **"Need help now?" now opens in Arabic for Arabic readers.** The crisis dialog and the button that opens it were rendering in English, left-to-right, on every page in the Arabic app — the one place in Femio where reading the words matters most, and the Arabic wording had been written and reviewed months ago. The dedicated crisis page was always correct, which is why it went unnoticed. Nothing about what Femio says in a crisis has changed; it now reaches the people it was written for.
- **The doctor-visit summary prints properly.** The box marking what Femio worked out — as opposed to what you logged — was effectively invisible on paper: printing from dark mode lost its heading entirely, and printing from light mode lost its dashed outline. A clinician reading the printout could not tell an estimate from something you recorded, which is the whole reason that box exists. The printed page now has its own black-and-grey palette instead of borrowing the app's.
- **Your average cycle length now comes from your recent cycles.** It used to include everything you had ever logged, so a long gap or a cycle from two years ago could drag it somewhere that no longer describes you — and a gap between periods could be averaged in as though it were a cycle. Your dashboard and your doctor-visit summary now give the same number.
- **If you skipped the age question, the privacy switches now explain themselves.** Turning on analytics or research quietly failed for anyone who chose "I'm not sure" during setup — Femio treats an unknown age as under 18 and will not share anything for a minor. It now says so, and lets you answer the age question right there instead of leaving you stuck.
- **Logging every day of your period no longer confuses Femio.** If you tapped through Monday, Tuesday and Wednesday while bleeding, Femio counted three separate cycles and told you your cycle was one day long. It now reads those as one period, the way you meant it.
- **A period logged without signal now appears as soon as you are back online.** It was always saved and always synced — but the screen kept saying "No periods logged yet" until you reloaded the page, which looked exactly like losing it.
- **The doctor-visit summary works again.** The page you print and take to an appointment had been failing to load since it was built, and told you it was your connection. It was ours: it asked the database for a field by the wrong name. Your record was never affected.
- **Nothing covers the privacy choices during sign-up any more.** The "Need help now?" button sat on top of one of the consent checkboxes on a small screen, so an option you were being asked to agree to could not be fully read.
- **Honest wording when something fails to load.** Two screens blamed your internet connection for faults on our side. They now say plainly that it is ours, and ask you to try again.

### Added
- **The whole library is live.** Femio now has 105 referenced guides covering periods and cycles, PCOS and endometriosis, trying to conceive, pregnancy week by week from week 4 to week 40, the weeks after birth, newborn basics, the teenage years, and perimenopause and menopause — every one sourced to bodies like WHO, NICE, ACOG, CDC and the NHS, and every one reachable from the app and from the web without an account.
- **The page behind the only warning in the menopause experience now exists.** If your periods stopped more than a year ago and you bleed — one spot, brown discharge, anything — Femio already showed you a standing note to get it checked. Now there is a guide behind it: why every episode is checked however small, what the appointment usually involves, what to say when you book, and why being told it is something minor is a good outcome rather than a wasted visit.
- **Ten guides written for teenagers** — what is changing in your body, what a period actually is, getting ready for a first one, the myths checked against real guidance, whether your period is normal, choosing between pads, tampons, cups and period underwear, what helps with pain and when it is too much, getting through a school day, tracking without it becoming another worry, and when to tell an adult or see a doctor. Written for readers of about nine to seventeen, in plainer language than the rest of the library. **No condition is ever named as something you might have** — irregular periods in the first years are what usually happens, not a finding — and nothing predicts when a first period will arrive, because nobody can. Every one is sourced to ACOG, the NHS, the NIH, the FDA or the American Academy of Pediatrics, and every one is waiting on a clinician approving it before it appears.
- **Eight guides about your newborn**, written for you rather than about your baby: the first two weeks, feeding and how it is checked, the warning signs that need a doctor today, crying and what helps, safer sleep, nappies and skin and the cord stump, preparing bottles and formula safely, and the checks and vaccinations usually offered. Not a baby tracker — no milestones, no growth charts. **Nothing in them judges your baby.** Whether *your* child is feeding enough, growing well or sleeping normally is a question for a midwife, health visitor or paediatrician, and every page says so in its first lines and again at the end. Where countries genuinely disagree — bed sharing, how formula should be made up, when vaccinations start — Femio says so and cites both rather than pretending there is one answer. Sourced to the NHS, NICE, WHO, CDC and the American Academy of Pediatrics, and every one is waiting on a clinician approving it before it appears.
- **Pregnancy week by week, weeks 4 to 40 — thirty-seven guides, one for every week.** What is happening to your body, what you may notice and which of it needs care today rather than at the next appointment, what is happening for your baby with the size comparisons people actually enjoy, one specific thing worth doing this week, and — where the week genuinely has one — the scan, screening offer, vaccination or test usually offered around then, always attributed and always saying that schedules differ between countries. **The size comparisons describe babies in general and never yours.** Every figure names where it came from, because a woman whose scan measured small should not be handed a verdict by an app, and there are no weights at all: the per-week weight tables everywhere on the internet trace back to app blogs rather than to anyone who would stand behind them. There is no kick count either. From the week your baby's movements are established, every page says the thing that actually matters — any change from *your* baby's usual pattern, checked by ringing your maternity unit, at any hour. Weeks 1 to 3 are deliberately absent, because pregnancy is dated from your last period and those weeks come before conception. Sourced throughout to the NHS, ACOG, RCOG, the NIH and the CDC, and every one is waiting on a clinician approving it before it appears.
- **Your pregnancy reading list now knows what week you are in.** It used to be the same three links for everyone from the first week to the last. Now the guide for the week you are actually in sits at the top of it and moves with you, and the rest of the card is drawn from what Femio knows about you rather than from a fixed list. Past your due date it holds the week 40 guide rather than running out, because that is the one you are looking for.
- **What you told Femio about your health now follows you past the birth.** If you said you had gestational diabetes, high blood pressure, pre-eclampsia or a thyroid condition during pregnancy, the after-birth experience keeps that in view instead of starting from nothing — including the follow-up appointments that routinely get dropped once the attention moves to the baby. If you told us about your mood, the tone and the reading change to match. **Femio decides none of this from a questionnaire score**: it uses what you chose to tell it, and you can change your answers at any time in Settings.
- **Fourteen guides for after the birth** — your body in the first six weeks, bleeding, warning signs, healing after stitches or a caesarean, afterpains, the pelvic floor, mood, sleep and exhaustion, the first two weeks of breastfeeding, what to do when breastfeeding is not working, when periods come back, contraception, the six-week check, and sex after birth. Every one is sourced to WHO, NICE, ACOG or CDC, and every one is waiting on a clinician's approval before it appears — nothing publishes automatically.
- **The after-birth dashboard now has a reading list**, and it is built from what Femio knows about you rather than a fixed set of links. It shows only guides that have actually been reviewed and published, so it can never send you to a page that is not there, and it fills in on its own as more are approved.
- **What Femio says after a pregnancy ends now depends on what happened** (P2.3). It has always recorded whether a pregnancy ended in a birth, a loss, or something you chose not to name — and it has always treated all three the same, which meant a woman who had just recorded a stillbirth saw the same words as one who had just had a baby. Now there are three separate messages, written separately. A birth is congratulated, once, and offered recovery and feeding. **A loss gets nothing cheerful, nothing hurried, and no countdown** — only that your record is safe and that Femio will move when you want it to, and no feeding content appears anywhere. And if you ended the record without saying how, Femio assumes **neither**: no congratulations and no condolences on the strength of a question you chose not to answer. Nothing switches on its own in any of the three.
- **Femio notices when things change, and asks** (P1.27.6). Record a pregnancy while you are tracking to conceive, and it offers to switch — it does not just switch. End a pregnancy, and it offers to move to the after-birth view, gently and with no hurry. Six months on from a birth it offers to move you back to cycle tracking. Every one of these is a question with a **No thanks** next to it, because a life stage is not something an app gets to decide about you. Saying no to one suggestion never silences a different one later. And Femio will not raise a pregnancy that ended long ago: that would be the app bringing something up unprompted, months later, possibly while someone is looking over your shoulder.
- **Signup asks one more thing, and only when it can use the answer** (P1.27.8). If you are trying to conceive, Femio asks how long — because that changes what is useful to put in front of you, and it is the one thing it can never work out from what you log: it started before you had the app. If you have recently given birth, it asks roughly when. That second question matters more than it looks: most people install Femio *after* the birth, so without it the app had no idea where you were and quietly gave you a general cycle screen. Both are bands rather than dates — roughly is enough — and skipping either assumes nothing.
- **The two mood questionnaires your doctor already uses** (P2.7): PHQ-9 and GAD-7, in all four languages, in Settings and available whatever life stage you are in. Same rule as the others — Femio gives you the number and does not grade it. And the same exception: if you say you have had thoughts of being better off dead or of hurting yourself, ways to get help open the moment you tap it, whatever the rest of your answers add up to.
- **Femio can now offer to tailor itself, instead of waiting to be told** (P1.27.5). If you skipped the life-stage question — or were not sure — Femio watches what you actually log and, once it has enough to go on, asks: *would you like this set up for [stage]?* It names a life stage in plain words, it says you can change it back before you decide, and if you say no it does not ask again. It stays quiet when it does not really know: an account with nothing logged gets no suggestion at all, because a guess presented as personalization is worse than a blank.
- **The postpartum mood questionnaire, and the one answer Femio acts on** (P2.7). Ten questions from the Edinburgh scale — the one a midwife or health visitor is most likely to hand you — in all four languages, with a total you can save and show. As with the menopause questionnaire, Femio gives you the number and does not grade it. **The exception is the last question.** If you say the thought of harming yourself has occurred to you, ways to get help open straight away: the moment you tap it, not when you finish, and no matter how low the rest of your answers are. That is deliberate — someone can answer that question honestly and still score below every threshold in the research. Saving is separate and optional, because you should never have to leave a record behind to be offered help.
- **A contraction timer and a weight chart** (P2.2.3). The timer keeps how long each one lasted and how far apart they were — the two numbers you get asked for on the phone — and **that is all it does.** It never says "this looks like labour", because deciding that from a stopwatch is not something an app should do, and because the version of that mistake which hurts is the one that tells a woman "not yet". The number to call sits above the timer the whole time, not after some threshold. The weight chart draws your readings as dots with **no target line and no shaded range**: guidance about weight gain exists, but drawn behind your own numbers it becomes a judgement about your body rendered as a background colour, every single week.
- **One list of everything you take** (P2.4): HRT, the pill, thyroid tablets, iron, a prenatal vitamin — in one place you can read out at an appointment, whatever life stage you are in. Femio **never suggests a dose, never checks one, and never scores you on how well you keep to it.** The first is your prescriber's job; the last is the thing medication apps do that makes a bad week feel like a failure. When you stop something it does not disappear — it moves down the screen, because "I used to be on this" is one of the most useful things you can tell a doctor.
- **Nineteen more things you can log** (P2.4): night sweats, vaginal dryness, needing the loo urgently, tingling hands, a burning mouth, ringing ears, sore gums, brittle nails, sudden panicky feelings — the experiences of perimenopause that most lists leave out, plus the ones people are embarrassed to raise. **All of them are available in every mode**, not just menopause: your life stage decides what Femio puts in front of you, never what you are allowed to record. What is deliberately *not* on the list is anything that is a diagnosis rather than a feeling — the widely-shared "34 symptoms of menopause" includes osteoporosis and allergies, and inviting you to tick those would be inviting you to diagnose yourself.
- **A menopause questionnaire your doctor already knows** (P2.4): eleven questions from the Menopause Rating Scale, in plain language and in all four languages, with a total and three subscores you can save and show at an appointment. Femio gives you the number and **deliberately does not grade it** — no "mild", no "severe", no colour. The published research does attach thresholds to these totals; applying them would be Femio assessing you, and the person that hurts is the one who reads "mild" and decides the appointment can wait. If you skip questions it says so, because a total from six answers is not the same as a total from eleven and it would be dishonest to present them as if they were.
- **Charting is here: waking temperature, ovulation tests, and cervical mucus** (P2.1). Your temperatures are drawn as your own chart — and drawn as **dots and nothing else**. No line through them, no marker saying "you ovulated here", no shaded band. That is deliberate and permanent: reading a temperature chart is a clinical judgement made from a home thermometer, it belongs to you and your clinician, and an app that made it would be one small step from telling you which days are "safe" — which no cycle app can honestly do. Femio records what your ovulation test said; it does not read the test for you. The screen says all of this at the top, in one sentence, before you log anything. Charting is in Settings and works in any mode, because plenty of people chart without calling themselves "trying".
- **You can keep your appointments in Femio now** (P2.2.3): when it is, who it is with, and — the part that actually gets lost — **the questions you meant to ask**, so they are in your hand when you are in the room. If you were told to expect a result by a date and that date has gone by, Femio quietly says it might be worth asking; it never suggests what the answer will be. There is deliberately **nowhere to record what was found**. Femio holds your appointments, not your results: the moment an app starts keeping "scan — normal", it has started keeping a clinical opinion, and that belongs in your notes with the person who formed it. Appointments live in Settings and are reachable in every mode — a fertility work-up, a gynae follow-up and a menopause clinic are all appointments, not pregnancy ones.
- **Femio has a mode for teenagers now** (R2.5c): "I'm a teenager" is the first choice at signup, and it changes the app rather than decorating it. The next period is shown as a **wide window with the reason attached** — cycles jump around for the first year or two, and an app that says "Tuesday" and is wrong teaches a 13-year-old that either it is broken or she is. Before her first period there is **no prediction at all**, because there is nothing honest to predict. Anything worth mentioning to a parent or a doctor is said calmly, in an ordinary panel, never in a red alert — irregular cycles at that age are what usually happens, not a finding. And nothing is hidden: the whole library is one tap away and the screen says so. Teen mode governs ads, data and social exposure, never what she is allowed to read. Until now a thirteen-year-old got the adult cycle screen.

### Changed
- **Every guide now says who wrote it and who checked it** (P1.9.8): all 30 health pages carry a named clinician with credentials — on the page, and in the structured data search engines read — linked to a bio you can open. The homepage says so too, as the first of six things you can verify. It did not say so before, and that was deliberate: the line was written, then removed, because at the time no page actually carried a byline and a promise you cannot check on the page itself is worse than no promise. The one page that gets an author and **no** medical reviewer is "Meet Femio" — it explains the product rather than your health, and a review stamp there would spend the credential where it means nothing.

- **The app looks and behaves like an app now — starting with the way you move around it and the first thing you see** (P1.28.1/P1.28.2, ADR-051): the three text links at the top have become a proper bottom tab bar with icons, where your thumb already is. Signing out moved into Settings, where it belongs, instead of sitting one mis-tap away from Track. And your home screen no longer opens with two identical-looking cards asking you to work out which one matters: it opens with the answer — the day of your cycle, when your next period is expected, and **a bar that draws that window rather than just naming it**, so a wide, uncertain estimate visibly looks wide. Under it, in plain words, is how confident that estimate is: Femio has been working this out since the beginning and had never shown it to you. There is still no countdown and still no "you are X days late", in any mode — if the window passes, Femio says so, says cycles shift, and leaves the conclusion where it belongs. Everything was checked on a phone-sized screen, in English, in Arabic right-to-left, and in dark mode.

- **Logging is built for a thumb now** (P1.28.3, ADR-052): the day you are logging for sits at the top of the Track screen as a row of days you can tap — today, yesterday, any of the last two weeks — instead of a date box buried in the middle of a form. Days you have already recorded a period on are marked. The symptom buttons are large enough to hit without looking and respond when you press them. The date field is still there for anything older, and it and the day row are always the same choice. Saving now tells you it saved.

- **The pregnancy and menopause home screens got the same treatment** (P1.28.4, ADR-053): pregnancy now shows the weeks you are, and a bar that draws **full term — 37 to 42 weeks** — with today marked inside it. The old bar filled toward your due date, which made that one date look like a finish line; most babies do not arrive on it, and the five-week range is both the truer picture and the kinder one. Menopause gets the same clear layout and, on purpose, **no bar at all**: a progress track filling toward the twelve-month mark would look like a countdown to a diagnosis, and that is a conclusion for you and your clinician, not for an app.

- **Buttons respond when you press them, and the app respects "reduce motion"** (P1.28.6, ADR-055): tapping anything in Femio used to do nothing visible until whatever it triggered finished — on a slow connection, that is indistinguishable from a broken button. Every button now answers a touch immediately. And if you have asked your phone or computer for less motion, Femio now honours it everywhere, including the pulsing placeholders that appear while your data loads. Two things that had been left behind also caught up: the symptom buttons in pregnancy mode are now the same comfortable size as the ones in cycle mode, and the life-stage picker in Settings now behaves like the one in signup.

- **Signing up looks and works like the first screen of a real app** (P1.28.5, ADR-054): the Femio name is on it now — it wasn't — along with a progress bar showing how short it is, proper radio buttons so you can see which choices are choices before you tap one, and a Continue button pinned to the bottom of the screen at full width instead of tucked in the corner after a long scroll. Screen readers now hear "option 2 of 4" instead of "button, pressed", which is what these controls actually are.

Femio's dependency report is back to **zero known issues** — checked at a stricter level than the build requires.

That screen also kept showing stale entries after saving. Both are fixed, and pregnancy logging now behaves exactly like cycle logging, which is what it always looked like it was already doing.

Your entries now appear the moment they are saved.

- **The "Need help now?" button was hidden behind the new tab bar** (ADR-051): found by looking at a real phone screen rather than a desktop browser, and fixed before it shipped. ### Added
- **You can now change how Femio is set up for you, whenever your life changes** (P1.27.1, ADR-050): Settings has a new "Your Femio experience" section. Pick cycle, trying to conceive, pregnancy, or perimenopause/menopause — or choose **General**, which assumes nothing about your stage and simply gives you everything. Until now the question was asked once, during signup, and there was no way back: a wrong answer was permanent, and a real change — becoming pregnant, no longer being pregnant, periods stopping — had no way of reaching the app. **Changing it never deletes anything.** Your record stays whole, every tool stays available, and switching back finds it exactly as you left it. Femio's promise is one record across a whole life, and this is the control that makes that true rather than merely stated.
- **A movement log for pregnancy — and deliberately not a "count to ten" kick counter** (P2.2, ADR-049): you can time a session and tap each time you feel your baby move. What Femio does with it is show you *your own* pattern: how far apart movements were this time compared with your recent sessions. What it will never do is tell you a number is enough. The guidance Femio publishes — and follows — is that there is no normal number of movements for everyone, and that what matters is a change from your baby's usual pattern. A counter that congratulates you for reaching ten can reassure exactly the person who should be phoning, so this one has no target and no finish line. The line telling you to contact your maternity team if movements are less than usual sits **above** the counter and stays there the whole time: it never asks you to finish counting first. You can stop a session at any point and it still saves, because stopping to make a phone call is the most important thing this could record. Works offline, is included in your data export, and is deleted with your account.

### Changed
- **The tracker screens load faster, and they can now become real phone apps** (P4.0, ADR-048): every screen you use when signed in — your dashboard, logging, settings, the visit summary — was being rebuilt by a server on each visit. They are now delivered as a ready-made page that fetches your data straight from the database in your browser. Two things follow. The pages arrive quicker, because the server no longer has to assemble them first. And, more importantly, the same screens can now be packaged into the iOS and Android apps: a phone app has no server behind it, so the old design could never have shipped to a store — something nobody had discovered because nothing had tried to build for a phone yet. Nothing about what the screens show has changed. One visible addition: if your data cannot be loaded, you now get a clear message and a retry button instead of a page that looks empty — because "you have logged nothing" and "we could not reach your records" are very different things to tell someone about her own health record.
- **Every page got about a third lighter** (P1.11.2, ADR-037): Femio was sending the *entire* set of translations to your browser on every page — the privacy policy, the terms, the two-step-sign-in copy and the staff dashboard, on a page showing none of them. Now only the text a page can actually use is sent. A guide page dropped from 62 KB to 40 KB, and Arabic — which costs more bytes per character — from a larger figure to 46 KB. That is a real difference on a slow phone or a metered connection, and pages that load faster also rank better, which matters for a site people find through search. A check now runs on every change so it cannot quietly creep back.

### Fixed
- **The content CMS was returning a server error, and the cause was a question nobody answered** (ADR-030): the guides API had been down since the publishing-fields deploy, because the database change that deploy needed had never actually run. The migration tool looked like it was hanging — five deploys had each burned Vercel's 45-minute build limit, and it behaved the same way locally, so it had been ruled a broken tool and taken out of the build. It was not hanging. It prints a yes/no safety question and waits for someone to type an answer, and in an automated build there is nobody there; 45 minutes was the build system giving up, not the tool running. Answered, it finishes in **under two seconds**. The API is back to normal, and there is now a documented command that answers the question so this cannot happen again.
- Closed the last known vulnerable dependencies (P1.23.2, ADR-022): upgrading the translation library clears an **open-redirect** flaw — the kind used to bounce someone from a trusted address to a malicious one — plus a second issue. Femio's dependency report is now at **zero known advisories**, from fifteen (one critical) at the start of this security pass.
- **Unknown article URLs returned a server error instead of "page not found"** (P1.2.2 / ADR-021): any stale, mistyped or renamed link under `/learn`, `/conditions` or `/authors` produced a 500 in production — bad for readers and worse for search engines, which log it as a site fault rather than a missing page. It had been live for weeks and was invisible in local development, which renders pages differently. Fixed, and Femio now has a proper localized 404 page in all four languages (right-to-left in Arabic) that offers a way back to the homepage or the library.
- The claims-language guard could report a false pass: run from anywhere but the repo root it found no message catalogs, scanned nothing, and still printed its ✓. It now fails loudly instead — this is the check that keeps Femio's copy out of medical-device scope, so silence had to mean "clean", not "didn't look".
- CMS publishing was impossible — no admin account existed (ADR-018, → apps/cms/scripts/set-user-roles.mjs): the content CMS had a single staff account holding only the `editor` role, and roles can only be changed by an admin, so nobody could approve or publish content or manage users (Payload's "create first user" screen skips the admin-only rule but still applies the editor default). Added a small, reviewable bootstrap script — `pnpm --filter cms set-roles` — that repairs an account's roles (and can reset a lost CMS password) through Payload's own API, so hooks, validation and password hashing behave exactly as in the admin panel. One command unblocks publishing (runbook C3.7).
- Critical client-side hydration crash (all signed-in pages): `env.ts` validated the whole `process.env`, which Next.js leaves empty in the client bundle, so validation threw and `/welcome`, `/track`, `/dashboard`, and `/settings` crashed on hydration in production (SSR/curl couldn't detect it). Now references each `NEXT_PUBLIC_*` var explicitly. Caught by the new Playwright e2e harness and verified fixed on live prod.

### Added
- **A content guard that reads every article draft against the authoring brief** (P1.9.1, ADR-031, → `scripts/content-lint.mjs`): checking sixty health articles by hand is slow and gets less careful as it goes — and the checks that matter most are exactly the mechanical ones attention fails at: a missing top-tier source, an invented reviewer name, a page title that collides with another page. One command now covers all of it — front-matter completeness, drafts-only status, claim language wherever *Femio* is the actor, at least two sources with one top-tier and no blogs or Wikipedia, uniqueness of every title and description across the whole set, internal links resolved against the site's **real routes** rather than a list someone has to remember to update, the required shape of a page (a direct answer first, a "When to see a doctor" section, a red-flag box wherever a symptom can be an emergency), health-not-medical wording, crisis-safety routing, and a ban on phone numbers. It deliberately does **not** judge medicine: whether a claim is true and complete stays with the clinician, and the guard exists so nothing mechanical wastes that reviewer's time. A separate self-test hands each rule an article built to break it and fails if the rule stays silent — a guard nobody has watched fail is indistinguishable from no guard — and it immediately caught a real bug in the guard itself.
- **A way to get articles into the CMS without losing half of them** (P1.9.2, ADR-031, → `apps/cms/scripts/import-articles.mjs`): until now the only route was a person pasting articles into the admin panel one at a time, which silently drops the fields that make a health page trustworthy — its sources, its search description, its disclaimer type, its author and reviewer. The importer carries all of that across, and refuses anything it cannot carry faithfully rather than creating a page that looks complete and is not. Everything arrives as a **draft**: publication stays a clinician's decision behind the review gate, and the importer has no way to override it.
- Articles can now carry a real byline (P1.6.6): the CMS gained public author and medical-reviewer fields linked to the bio pages, plus dedicated search and social fields (title, description, canonical, social image) and a first-published date. Published guides show who wrote them and which clinician reviewed them, with credentials and links to their profiles — and search engines get the same in structured data. Editors can write a page's search title and description separately from its headline.
- Error monitoring that can't see your health data (P1.4.2, ADR-026): Femio now reports crashes to Sentry so we find faults before you report them — but the reports are cut down first. The page address is replaced by its *shape* (`/[locale]/learn/[slug]`), so we know which page broke without learning which article you were reading; and anything you had just typed, your cookies and your identity are removed entirely. Session recording is off. Reports go to EU servers only — a non-EU address fails the build on purpose.
- Product analytics now actually reports — for people who opt in (P1.20.1/P1.20.4, ADR-025/ADR-027): the PostHog connection is live behind the consent toggle. Two things are worth knowing about how it is built. The analytics code is **not even downloaded** until someone turns the toggle on — not loaded-and-quiet, genuinely not fetched. And the page address is stripped from every event before it is sent: a URL like `/learn/endometriosis-symptoms` says something about your health, so it never leaves your browser, along with referrers and campaign tags. What is sent is a coarse area name (“library”, “calculators”) and nothing else — each page states its own area as a fixed label, so there is no code anywhere that could turn a web address into an analytics event. Sign-up and sign-in are counted by method and outcome only, never with your life stage, email or identity; calculator use is counted once per visit and never says which calculator, because “due date” and “fertile window” would each imply something about your health.
- A proper error page when something breaks (P1.2.4, ADR-024): if a page fails unexpectedly, Femio now shows a branded, translated screen — in all four languages, right-to-left in Arabic — that says your logged data is safe and offers a retry or a way home, instead of the framework's bare error text.
- Two-step sign-in (P1.23.3, ADR-023, → Settings → "Two-step sign-in"): you can now protect your account with a code from an authenticator app on top of your password — scan a QR code, confirm one code, done. It's **required for staff accounts**: anyone with back-office access must pass the second step before the translation portal or the insights dashboard will open, so a stolen staff password isn't enough on its own. Available in all four languages.
- Security hardening of the site itself (P1.23.1, ADR-021): every page now ships a strict set of browser security headers — a Content-Security-Policy whose connection allow-list is built from the services Femio actually uses (so a page cannot talk to an unlisted third party at all), a ban on embedding Femio in another site's frame, HSTS, a referrer policy that never leaks which article you were reading, and a permissions policy switching off camera, microphone, location and payments. Dependency security is now enforced in CI: known-vulnerable packages fail the build, the report went from 15 advisories (one critical) to 3, and the one remaining accepted issue is written down with a reason and a review date.
